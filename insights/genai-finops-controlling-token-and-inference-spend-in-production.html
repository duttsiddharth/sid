GenAI FinOps: Controlling Token and Inference Spend in Production

Siddharth DuttNov 20268 min read
In this article
  1. Where the GenAI Bill Actually Comes From
  2. Why Cost Is a Governance Problem, Not an Engineering One
  3. The Controls I Put in Place

Through 2025, most enterprises I worked with were running GenAI in pilots — a contained proof of value, a budget line nobody scrutinised too hard because the numbers were small. In 2026 that changed. The pilots went to production, usage scaled with real users, and the first serious invoices landed on finance's desk. The question stopped being "does this work?" and became "why did this cost what it cost, and who signed off on that?"

That second question is the one most teams are unprepared for. The spend is real, it's variable, and — unlike a server you provisioned — it moves every time someone changes a prompt, adds a retrieval step, or lets an agent loop one more time. Treating that as purely an engineering optimisation misses the point. It's a governance gap.

Where the GenAI Bill Actually Comes From

Before you can control GenAI spend, you have to know what actually drives it. In the production systems I've reviewed, the cost almost never comes from where people assume. It comes from a handful of repeatable patterns:

  • Context bloat. A prompt that started lean grows over time — more system instructions, more examples, a larger retrieved context window — and every token in that context is paid for on every single call.
  • Model mismatch. A frontier-tier model doing work a smaller, cheaper model would handle just as well. The default is often "use the best model everywhere," which is the most expensive possible starting point.
  • Unbounded retrieval. A single RAG call that pulls far more documents than the answer needs, quietly multiplying the token count on the way into the model.
  • Retries and agent loops. Automatic retries, multi-step agents, and self-correction passes each re-pay for the full context. A loop with no ceiling is a cost incident waiting to happen.
  • No attribution. Spend that can't be traced to a team, feature, or use case — so nobody owns it, and nobody can tell which part is actually earning its keep.

The pattern underneath all of these is the same one I've seen with cloud spend for years: a small number of use cases typically account for most of the bill, and you can't fix what you can't see.

"GenAI cost doesn't blow up with a bang. It creeps — one extra retrieval, one richer prompt, one more agent step at a time — until the monthly invoice is writing your roadmap for you."

Why Cost Is a Governance Problem, Not an Engineering One

Engineers can make any single call cheaper. What they can't do from inside the code is decide which use cases are worth running, who is accountable for a budget, or what happens when spend crosses a threshold. Those are governance decisions, and they belong in the same operating model as your other AI risk controls — not in a backlog ticket.

This is why I file cost under AI resilience and governance rather than pure FinOps. A system you can't afford to run at scale is just as unavailable as one that's technically down — the business outcome is identical. If an unexpected invoice forces you to throttle or switch off a GenAI feature mid-quarter, that's a resilience failure dressed up as a finance problem. Designing for cost is part of designing for operations that keep running.

The Controls I Put in Place

The goal isn't to spend less for its own sake — it's to make spend visible, owned, and bounded, so the business can make deliberate trade-offs instead of discovering them on an invoice. The controls that consistently work:

  • Attribution first. Before optimising anything, tag every call with the team, feature, and use case behind it. Until you can see spend by use case, every optimisation is a guess.
  • Budgets with teeth. Per-use-case budgets with alerts at a warning threshold and a hard behaviour — degrade, queue, or fall back — at the ceiling. A budget nobody enforces is just a hope.
  • Model routing. Run at least two tiers and route by task: a small, cheap model for classification and routine work, a frontier model reserved for the cases that genuinely need it. This single decision moves the bill more than any prompt tweak.
  • Context discipline. Treat the context window as a budget, not a bucket. Trim system prompts, cap retrieved documents, and measure tokens-per-call as a first-class metric.
  • Caching and reuse. Cache repeated prompts and prompt prefixes so you stop paying full price for the same work twice.
  • Loop ceilings. Every agent, retry, and self-correction path gets a hard maximum and a cost guardrail, so a misbehaving loop fails safe instead of failing expensive.
Key Takeaway

Make GenAI spend visible and owned before you try to make it smaller. Attribution, budgets with real behaviour at the ceiling, and routing work to the cheapest model that can do the job will do more for your bill — and your resilience — than any amount of prompt micro-tuning.

The organisations getting durable value from GenAI in production aren't the ones spending the least. They're the ones who can say, with confidence, exactly what they're spending, on which use cases, and why — and who decided that on purpose rather than at the mercy of a monthly invoice.
